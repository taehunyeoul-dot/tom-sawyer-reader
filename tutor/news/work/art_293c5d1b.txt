A Sunflower’s View of Totality
NASA · 2026-08-14
https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/a-sunflowers-view-of-totality/

[0.0] About Search Skywatchers across a swath of northern Spain witnessed a total solar eclipse when the Moon lined up directly between the Sun and Earth on August 12, 2026.
[0.1] Those within the path of totality had the rare chance to glimpse the Sun’s active outer atmosphere, or corona , during the short time the Moon blocked the Sun.

[1.0] This composite photo was taken before, during, and after the total eclipse from a field of appropriately themed flowers in San Millán de los Caballeros, a town about 40 kilometers (25 miles) south of León in northwestern Spain.
[1.1] Along with parts of Greenland and Iceland, Northern Spain was one of the few places on land that fell within this eclipse’s path of totality.

[2.0] Sunset was approaching when the Moon’s shadow, or umbra, swept across Spain.
[2.1] In León, the partial eclipse began at 7:32 p.m. and ended at 9:22 p.m. local time, just minutes before the Sun dipped below the horizon.
[2.2] Totality lasted about two minutes, starting at 8:28 p.m. Farther east in Spain, the Sun set before the eclipse ended.

[3.0] In the photo above, taken during totality, a glowing loop of plasma called a solar prominence is visible extending into the corona on the left.
[3.1] Plasma , a super-hot gas composed mostly of ionized hydrogen and helium, flows along the tangled and twisted structure of the Sun’s magnetic fields.
[3.2] Solar prominences, which can measure many times higher than Earth is wide, are sometimes visible to the naked eye during eclipses.

[4.0] Solar eclipses offer NASA the opportunity to get a different look at the Sun and our own atmosphere.
[4.1] On August 12, science teams staged in Iceland to chase the Moon’s shadow in one of NASA’s WB-57 high-altitude jets and image the corona in visible and infrared light.
[4.2] And the NASA-supported Nationwide Eclipse Ballooning Project launched scientific balloons before, during, and after the eclipse to measure how Earth’s atmosphere changed when the Sun was temporarily blocked.

[5.0] While viewing opportunities in the path of totality were limited, the rest of Europe and parts of Africa, Canada, and the U.S. experienced a partial eclipse .
[5.1] Photos and video from the total and partial eclipse are available in NASA’s image library .
[5.2] The next total solar eclipse will occur on August 2, 2027, with the path of totality crossing southern Spain, North Africa, Saudi Arabia, and Yemen.
